What this image shows

On the left side, there is a bulletin board filled with papers marked "FALSE!" and skeptical people pointing at them, while a broom is sweeping these papers away; on the right side, a cheerful robed figure stands before a glowing mountain, raising their arms beside praising onlookers and a sign promising a miracle. The scene suggests that despite a long history of failed predictions being visibly removed or ignored, people are now inferring that a powerful miracle is about to happen simply based on the new claim. The important contrast is between the obvious evidence of prior failures and the sudden leap to renewed belief in an extraordinary event. We can see that the happy expressions, dramatic gestures, and glowing background do not erase the repeated, documented track record of past mistakes, so the claim lacks support from the visible facts alone.

Why it misleads

A fuller explanation of how the fallacy works and why it can look persuasive.

A track record does not make contrary outcomes impossible, but it does affect rational priors. Ignoring repeated past failure forces every new case to borrow more credibility than it has earned.

That's like saying...

Instead of leading with the label, this analogy answers the shape of the reasoning move directly so the mistake is easier to see in plain language.

Fallacious claim

Yes, the last hundred miracle predictions failed, but this new one starts with a perfectly clean slate.

That's like saying...

That's like handing a serial false alarmist a fresh siren every morning and pretending yesterday's empty evacuations tell you nothing. History of failure is being erased when it should affect default confidence.

Caveat

This label is easy to overuse. The point here is not to call every weak argument by this name, but to reserve it for the exact misstep it describes.

Common misapplication

Do not use this label simply because the evidence is incomplete. It applies when the argument claims more support than the evidence has actually earned.

Use the label only when...

Use this label only when each new claim is treated as if the relevant history of prior failures, hoaxes, or false alarms did not exist and should confer no default expectation at all. If the real problem is that someone treats a failure to find expected evidence as if it counted for nothing against the claim, even in a context where the claim should leave detectable traces, the better label is Absence of evidence fallacy.

Practice And Repair

Extra teaching tools that show why the fallacy is persuasive, what to look for, and how to correct it.

Why it matters

Why this mistake matters

Track-record reset threatens rationality because each new claim is treated as if the relevant history of prior failures, hoaxes, or false alarms did not exist and should confer no default expectation at all.

Main reasoning problem

Each new claim is treated as if the relevant history of prior failures, hoaxes, or false alarms did not exist and should confer no default expectation at all.

Why this kind of mistake matters

It overstates, understates, cherry-picks, or misallocates the force of evidence.

What to watch for

What to watch for

Watch the trigger, the shortcut, and the payoff. Trigger: the claim enters under the Evidential frame. Shortcut: It overstates, understates, cherry-picks, or misallocates the force of evidence. Payoff: the conclusion becomes easier to accept, reject, or ignore before the relevant comparison has been paid for.

Common warning signs

Warning signs: track-record reset often appears when a discussion uses evidential pressure to accelerate belief. Look for a move from a weaker observation to a stronger conclusion.

How to fix it

How to fix it
