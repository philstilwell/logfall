That's like saying...

Instead of leading with the label, this analogy answers the shape of the reasoning move directly so the mistake is easier to see in plain language.

Fallacious claim

One error in this report means the entire investigation is worthless.

That's like saying...

That's like throwing away the whole meal because one dish was oversalted. A flaw in part of the package is being treated as total failure of the whole.

Caveat

This label is easy to overuse. The point here is not to call every weak argument by this name, but to reserve it for the exact misstep it describes.

Common misapplication

Do not use this label every time someone sounds too confident, too skeptical, or too simplified. It applies when belief or doubt is being managed badly relative to what can responsibly be known.

Use the label only when...

Use this label only when support for part of a view, or problems with part of a view, are treated as if they force total acceptance or total rejection of the whole package. If the real problem is that the wording of a negative position is manipulated so that mere non-belief is treated as if it were the same thing as a strong positive denial, the better label is Artificial negation.

Why it matters

Why this mistake matters

All or nothing fallacy threatens rationality because support for part of a view, or problems with part of a view, are treated as if they force total acceptance or total rejection of the whole package.

Main reasoning problem

Support for part of a view, or problems with part of a view, are treated as if they force total acceptance or total rejection of the whole package.

Why this kind of mistake matters

It corrupts the calibration of belief, doubt, burden, uncertainty, or standards of evidence.

What to watch for

What to watch for

Watch the trigger, the shortcut, and the payoff. Trigger: the claim enters under the Epistemic frame with secondary pressure from Evidential. Shortcut: It corrupts the calibration of belief, doubt, burden, uncertainty, or standards of evidence. Payoff: the conclusion becomes easier to accept, reject, or ignore before the relevant comparison has been paid for.

Common warning signs

Warning signs: all or nothing fallacy often appears when a discussion uses epistemic pressure to accelerate belief. Look for a move from a weaker observation to a stronger conclusion.
