Contextomy

Occurs when words are selectively excerpted from their original context in a way that changes or distorts what the speaker meant.

That's like saying...

Instead of leading with the label, this analogy answers the shape of the reasoning move directly so the mistake is easier to see in plain language.

Fallacious claim

The scientist said the result was 'uncertain,' so he admitted the theory was collapsing.

That's like saying...

That's like quoting 'I approve' from the sentence 'I do not approve' and calling the clip accurate. A sliced excerpt is being treated as if it preserved the original meaning.

Caveat

This label is easy to overuse. The point here is not to call every weak argument by this name, but to reserve it for the exact misstep it describes.

Common misapplication

Do not use this label every time an argument feels unfair, heated, or evasive. It applies when the move really does distract from, pressure, or replace the reasoning at issue.

Use the label only when...

Use this label only when words are selectively excerpted from their original context in a way that changes or distorts what the speaker meant. If the real problem is that someone advances a bold, controversial claim, retreats under criticism to a weaker and easier-to-defend claim, and then returns to the stronger claim as if the weaker claim had defended it, the better label is Motte and bailey fallacy.

Why this mistake matters

Why this kind of mistake matters

What to watch for

What to watch for

Watch the trigger, the shortcut, and the payoff. Trigger: the claim enters under the Tactical frame with secondary pressure from Linguistic. Shortcut: It moves attention away from the claim's evidential status and toward a pressure tactic, distraction, or rhetorical maneuver. Payoff: the conclusion becomes easier to accept, reject, or ignore before the relevant comparison has been paid for.

Common warning signs

Warning signs: contextomy often appears when a discussion uses tactical pressure to accelerate belief. Look for a move from a weaker observation to a stronger conclusion. Also watch for aliases or nearby labels: quoting out of context.
