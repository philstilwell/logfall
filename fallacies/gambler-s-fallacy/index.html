Gambler's fallacy

Occurs when someone thinks past outcomes of independent events make a future independent outcome more or less likely than it really is.

Occurs when someone thinks past outcomes of independent events make a future independent outcome more or less likely than it really is.

Why it matters

Why this mistake matters

Gambler's fallacy threatens rationality because someone thinks past outcomes of independent events make a future independent outcome more or less likely than it really is.

Main reasoning problem

Someone thinks past outcomes of independent events make a future independent outcome more or less likely than it really is.

Why this kind of mistake matters

It makes quantities, probabilities, rates, or samples push confidence farther than the math permits.

What to watch for

What to watch for

Watch the trigger, the shortcut, and the payoff. Trigger: the claim enters under the Mathematical frame with secondary pressure from Causal. Shortcut: It makes quantities, probabilities, rates, or samples push confidence farther than the math permits. Payoff: the conclusion becomes easier to accept, reject, or ignore before the relevant comparison has been paid for.

Common warning signs

Warning signs: gambler's fallacy often appears when a discussion uses mathematical pressure to accelerate belief. Look for a move from a weaker observation to a stronger conclusion. Also watch for aliases or nearby labels: monte carlo fallacy, hot hand fallacy.

Case studies

Documented cases chosen to stress-test the pattern with named sources rather than loose hypotheticals.

People sometimes feel that after many routine flights or medical screenings in a row, a disaster or diagnosis is somehow 'due,' even though independent risks do not balance themselves that way.

In gambling and investing language, talk of a roulette table or slot machine being 'ready to pay out' often confuses a streaky pattern with a real shift in odds.
