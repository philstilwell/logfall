Wishful thinking

Occurs when a belief or decision is driven mainly by what would be pleasing, hopeful, or comforting if true rather than by what the evidence supports.

Why it matters

Why this mistake matters

Wishful thinking threatens rationality because a belief or decision is driven mainly by what would be pleasing, hopeful, or comforting if true rather than by what the evidence supports.

Main reasoning problem

A belief or decision is driven mainly by what would be pleasing, hopeful, or comforting if true rather than by what the evidence supports.

Why this kind of mistake matters

It lets fear, disgust, outrage, hope, shame, or loyalty produce a confidence shift not earned by evidence.

What to watch for

What to watch for

Watch the trigger, the shortcut, and the payoff. Trigger: the claim enters under the Emotional frame with secondary pressure from Epistemic, Evidential. Shortcut: It lets fear, disgust, outrage, hope, shame, or loyalty produce a confidence shift not earned by evidence. Payoff: the conclusion becomes easier to accept, reject, or ignore before the relevant comparison has been paid for.

Common warning signs

Warning signs: wishful thinking often appears when a discussion uses emotional pressure to accelerate belief. Look for a move from a weaker observation to a stronger conclusion.
