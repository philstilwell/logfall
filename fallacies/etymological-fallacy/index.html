Etymological fallacy

Occurs when a word's original or historical meaning is treated as if it controlled the word's present meaning.

Why it misleads

A fuller explanation of how the fallacy works and why it can look persuasive.

Words change through use. Present meaning is set by current linguistic convention, not by the oldest recoverable ancestor of the term.

Caveat

This label is easy to overuse. The point here is not to call every weak argument by this name, but to reserve it for the exact misstep it describes.

Common misapplication

Do not use this label just because wording could have been clearer. It applies when ambiguity, redefinition, or verbal drift is doing real argumentative work.

Use the label only when...

Use this label only when a word's original or historical meaning is treated as if it controlled the word's present meaning. If the real problem is that a key word or phrase slides between different meanings inside the same argument, creating the illusion of support, the better label is Equivocation.

What to watch for

Payoff: the conclusion becomes easier to accept, reject, or ignore before the relevant comparison has been paid for.

Common warning signs

Warning signs: etymological fallacy often appears when a discussion uses linguistic pressure to accelerate belief. Look for a move from a weaker observation to a stronger conclusion.
