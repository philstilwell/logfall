Bottom-up condemnation

Occurs when a negative generalization about a group is used as if it settled the character or behavior of a specific member of that group.

ConceptualEvidential

Definition

Occurs when a negative generalization about a group is used as if it settled the character or behavior of a specific member of that group.

Illustrative example

People in private equity only care about money, so the new board member from that industry is obviously going to gut the program.

Why this kind of mistake matters

What to watch for

What to watch for

Watch the trigger, the shortcut, and the payoff. Trigger: the claim enters under the Conceptual frame with secondary pressure from Evidential. Shortcut: It warps the conceptual map so that distinctions, boundaries, or levels of analysis mislead the inference. Payoff: the conclusion becomes easier to accept, reject, or ignore before the relevant comparison has been paid for.

Common warning signs

Warning signs: bottom-up condemnation often appears when a discussion uses conceptual pressure to accelerate belief. Look for a move from a weaker observation to a stronger conclusion. Also watch for aliases or nearby labels: a dicto simpliciter ad dictum secundum quid.

After scandals in policing, finance, or clergy, critics sometimes assume any individual in the profession personally shares the worst traits of the institution. The fallacy here is Bottom-up condemnation: a negative generalization about a group is used as if it settled the character or behavior of a specific member of that group. That matters here because even if a group tendency were real, it would not eliminate the need for evidence about the particular person under discussion. The better question is whether the category or definition still fits once the context or scale changes.
