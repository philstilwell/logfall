Straw man argument

Occurs when someone replaces an opponent's actual position with a weaker, more extreme, or simplified version and then refutes that easier target.

Why it misleads

A fuller explanation of how the fallacy works and why it can look persuasive.

A straw man can be created by exaggeration, selective quotation, or by ignoring the qualifying parts of the original claim. It is not enough that a reply is critical; the key problem is that the reply no longer addresses the real position.

Why this kind of mistake matters

It moves attention away from the claim's evidential status and toward a pressure tactic, distraction, or rhetorical maneuver.

What to watch for

What to watch for

Watch the trigger, the shortcut, and the payoff. Trigger: the claim enters under the Tactical frame. Shortcut: It moves attention away from the claim's evidential status and toward a pressure tactic, distraction, or rhetorical maneuver. Payoff: the conclusion becomes easier to accept, reject, or ignore before the relevant comparison has been paid for.

Common warning signs

Warning signs: straw man argument often appears when a discussion uses tactical pressure to accelerate belief. Look for a move from a weaker observation to a stronger conclusion.
