Straw man argument

Occurs when someone replaces an opponent's actual position with a weaker, more extreme, or simplified version and then refutes that easier target.

Occurs when someone replaces an opponent's actual position with a weaker, more extreme, or simplified version and then refutes that easier target.

Why it misleads

A fuller explanation of how the fallacy works and why it can look persuasive.

A straw man can be created by exaggeration, selective quotation, or by ignoring the qualifying parts of the original claim. It is not enough that a reply is critical; the key problem is that the reply no longer addresses the real position.

Case studies

Recent or representative examples that help stress-test the pattern in practice.

During the September 10, 2024 Harris-Trump debate, claims about abortion rights were repeatedly recast into the far more extreme charge that Democrats support killing babies after birth, which is not the position being defended.
Debates over campus speech and content moderation often turn a narrower argument about rules, incentives, or disclosure into the cartoon claim that one side 'wants censorship' or 'wants total chaos.'
