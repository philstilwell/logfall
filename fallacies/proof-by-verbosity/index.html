Fallacy profile

Proof by verbosity

Occurs when a claim is protected by an avalanche of words, side points, jargon, or branching assertions that overwhelm reasonable scrutiny and create the illusion of depth.

TacticalLinguistic

Definition

Occurs when a claim is protected by an avalanche of words, side points, jargon, or branching assertions that overwhelm reasonable scrutiny and create the illusion of depth.

Illustrative example

The theory must be right because nobody has time to answer all 87 points in my thread.

Why it misleads

That's like saying...

Instead of leading with the label, this analogy answers the shape of the reasoning move directly so the mistake is easier to see in plain language.

Fallacious claim

The theory must be right because nobody has time to answer all 87 points in my thread.

That's like saying...

That's like piling so much wrapping paper on an empty box that people assume there must be something substantial inside. Sheer volume is being mistaken for substance.

Caveat

This label is easy to overuse. The point here is not to call every weak argument by this name, but to reserve it for the exact misstep it describes.

Common misapplication

Do not use this label every time an argument feels unfair, heated, or evasive. It applies when the move really does distract from, pressure, or replace the reasoning at issue.

Use the label only when...

Use this label only when a claim is protected by an avalanche of words, side points, jargon, or branching assertions that overwhelm reasonable scrutiny and create the illusion of depth. If the real problem is that words are selectively excerpted from their original context in a way that changes or distorts what the speaker meant, the better label is Contextomy.

Exact difference: Proof by verbosity happens when a claim is protected by an avalanche of words, side points, jargon, or branching assertions that overwhelm reasonable scrutiny and create the illusion of depth. Motte and bailey fallacy happens when someone advances a bold, controversial claim, retreats under criticism to a weaker and easier-to-defend claim, and then returns to the stronger claim as if the weaker claim had defended it.

Why this mistake matters

Main reasoning problem

Why this kind of mistake matters

It moves attention away from the claim's evidential status and toward a pressure tactic, distraction, or rhetorical maneuver.

What to watch for

What to watch for

Watch the trigger, the shortcut, and the payoff. Trigger: the claim enters under the Tactical frame with secondary pressure from Linguistic. Shortcut: It moves attention away from the claim's evidential status and toward a pressure tactic, distraction, or rhetorical maneuver. Payoff: the conclusion becomes easier to accept, reject, or ignore before the relevant comparison has been paid for.

Common warning signs

Warning signs: proof by verbosity often appears when a discussion uses tactical pressure to accelerate belief. Look for a move from a weaker observation to a stronger conclusion. Also watch for aliases or nearby labels: argumentum verbosium, proof by intimidation.
