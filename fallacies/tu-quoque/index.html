Tu quoque

Occurs when criticism is answered not by engaging the issue, but by pointing to similar hypocrisy or wrongdoing elsewhere.

Occurs when criticism is answered not by engaging the issue, but by pointing to similar hypocrisy or wrongdoing elsewhere.

What this image shows

The image shows two angry men confronting each other, one holding a paper labeled "FAKE QUOTE" and the other pointing an accusing finger, as well as scenes of a politician at a podium and a person at a computer with words about lies and fake news. The further claim being inferred from these elements is that because one side has committed wrongdoing or dishonesty, any charges of similar behavior from the other side must be dismissed or are equally invalid. The important contrast is between the visible accusations and emotional gestures (pointing, shouting, holding papers) and the inferred idea that hypocrisy alone cancels out the criticism or proves innocence. We can see people accusing each other and pointing to others’ faults, but this alone does not logically demonstrate that the original charge is false or unworthy of consideration.

Why it misleads

A fuller explanation of how the fallacy works and why it can look persuasive.

That's like saying...

Instead of leading with the label, this analogy answers the shape of the reasoning move directly so the mistake is easier to see in plain language.

Fallacious claim

You cannot criticize my fabricated quote because your side spreads misinformation too.

That's like saying...

That's like dismissing a doctor's warning about smoking because the doctor smokes. The hypocrisy may be real, but it does not by itself make the warning false.

Caveat

This label is easy to overuse. The point here is not to call every weak argument by this name, but to reserve it for the exact misstep it describes.

Common misapplication

Do not use this label whenever hypocrisy is mentioned. Hypocrisy can matter when the issue is sincerity, consistency, or credibility; it becomes tu quoque when hypocrisy is treated as a refutation of the original claim. Hypocrisy can matter for trust, but it does not by itself answer whether the original claim, act, or argument was wrong.

Use the label only when...

Use this label only when criticism is answered not by engaging the issue, but by pointing to similar hypocrisy or wrongdoing elsewhere. If the real problem is that a claim is accepted or dismissed because of some irrelevant association rather than because of the merits of the claim itself, the better label is Association fallacy.

Why this mistake matters

Why this kind of mistake matters

What to watch for

What to watch for

Watch the trigger, the shortcut, and the payoff. Trigger: the claim enters under the Tactical frame with secondary pressure from Evidential. Shortcut: It moves attention away from the claim's evidential status and toward a pressure tactic, distraction, or rhetorical maneuver. Payoff: the conclusion becomes easier to accept, reject, or ignore before the relevant comparison has been paid for.

Common warning signs

Warning signs: tu quoque often appears when a discussion uses tactical pressure to accelerate belief. Look for a move from a weaker observation to a stronger conclusion. Also watch for aliases or nearby labels: appeal to hypocrisy, look who's talking.
